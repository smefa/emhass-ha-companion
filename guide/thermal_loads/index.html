    Thermal loads¶

    A thermal load is like a deferrable load, but its temperature is controlled instead of its run time — a heat pump, direct electric heating, an air conditioner. Add one from Settings → Devices & Services → EMHASS Companion → Add thermal load — a separate button from Add deferrable load.

    Add a thermal load¶

    • Name
    • Temperature sensor — the room (or tank) the settings below apply to. Strongly recommended: without it, every plan starts from a guessed temperature instead of the real one.
    • Heats or cools — whether running the load raises the temperature (a heat pump, a heater) or lowers it (an air conditioner).
    • Power when running — roughly what it draws while on, in W.
    • Comfort temperature — the temperature to hold while the comfort window (below) is active. May go above this when electricity is cheap, never below.
    • Setback temperature — the lowest acceptable temperature outside the comfort window.
    • Maximum temperature — a hard ceiling, at all times.
    • Comfort from / Comfort until — when the comfort temperature applies each day. May cross midnight.
    • Advanced settings — the collapsed section holds the three model settings below (Heating rate, Heat loss constant, Response delay). The defaults are a reasonable starting point.
    • Heating rate — degrees per hour at full power.
    • Heat loss constant — degrees lost per hour, per degree of difference to the outdoor temperature.
    • Response delay — how long after switching on before the temperature responds. An hour or more for underfloor heating in concrete, 0 for a radiator or fan.
    • Heat pump COP curve — optional, also under Advanced settings. For a heat pump whose efficiency drops in the cold, enter outdoor temperature and COP pairs, such as 7: 5.2, 0: 4.0, -10: 3.0. The first pair is the outdoor temperature where you measured the Heating rate. The heating rate is lowered to match the coldest hour of the forecast, so cold spells get more heating hours. This needs a Home Assistant weather entity or an entity attribute as the outdoor temperature source. Leave it empty to ignore it. Heating only.
    • Running sensor — optional; tells the optimiser whether the load is actually running.
    • Control entity — optional; if set, the integration switches this on and off to follow the plan, once you turn control on.

    Temperature targets accept 5-95 °C in both setup and the device controls. This is the input range; the room-heating defaults are unchanged. Higher targets do not switch models: a Room load retains its simple model with heat loss relative to outdoor temperature. Choose Water tank explicitly for water storage and estimated hot-water consumption.

    Its own device page¶

    Every answer above becomes a setting on the load's device page, changeable any time. A thermal load also keeps the same Enabled, time window (Earliest start / Latest finish / Restrict to a time window), Most starts per plan and Cost of starting settings as an ordinary deferrable load — but has none of the run-time-target ones (Hours needed per day, Must run in one unbroken block, Recurrence, Requested, Run now), since its demand is the comfort band, not a number of hours.

    It also gets one extra sensor:

    • Planned temperature — the room temperature the plan currently expects, with the full forecast in its forecast attribute.

    Edit thermal load in the integration's menu only asks for the things that aren't controls: Name, Temperature sensor, Heats or cools, Running sensor and Control entity. Everything else is on the load's own page. A Room, advanced heat pump load also edits its house and heat pump settings there (see below).

    Outdoor temperature¶

    Once you have at least one thermal load, Configure → Outdoor temperature appears, asking where the outdoor temperature forecast comes from — it's what the room drifts towards while the load is off. A source is required. A Room, advanced heat pump load, or a room with a Heat pump COP curve, needs one of the first two sources:

    • Home Assistant weather entity — hourly forecast via weather.get_forecasts. Pick one that actually offers hourly data; daily or twice-daily forecasts are too coarse to schedule heating against. The other two sources are under the collapsed Advanced settings section (it opens by itself if one of them is already selected).
    • Temperature forecast from an entity attribute — for a weather integration that exposes its forecast as a list-valued attribute instead of through an action, or a template sensor you build yourself.
    • EMHASS built-in (Open-Meteo) — no weather integration or API key needed, but requires your home's location to be set correctly under Home Assistant's Settings → System → Home information, and is only correct when the solar forecast is also EMHASS built-in.

    The follow-up screen asks for what that source needs. Home Assistant weather entity wants just a Weather entity. Temperature forecast from an entity attribute wants a Forecast entity, an Attribute name, a Timestamp field and a Temperature field — values must be in degrees Celsius, which is what EMHASS's thermal model assumes. EMHASS built-in asks for nothing.

    Room, advanced heat pump¶

    Choose Room, advanced heat pump under Add thermal load if you would rather describe your house than measure a heating rate. EMHASS then calculates the heat pump's efficiency (COP) and the house's heat demand from the outdoor forecast at each step. A cold night costs more heat and more electricity without any tuning. Use Room instead if you have measured a heating rate that works, or if the load cools.

    Before you start¶

    • An outdoor temperature source the Companion can read: a Home Assistant weather entity or a Temperature forecast from an entity attribute. EMHASS built-in (Open-Meteo) is not enough, because the Companion needs the forecast itself. Without one, no plan is made and the error names the load.
    • EMHASS 0.18.8 or newer.
    • A rough idea of the house's yearly heat demand. An energy certificate, or last year's delivered heat divided by the heated floor area, is a good start. Delivered heat is not the electricity bought: multiply the heat pump's yearly electricity use by its seasonal COP.

    The setup form¶

    • Room temperature sensor — required. Every plan starts from this reading.
    • Electrical power when running — what the heat pump draws while it runs, in W. EMHASS multiplies it by the calculated COP to get the heat delivered.
    • Comfort / Setback / Maximum temperature and Comfort starts / ends — the same band as for a Room load. Setback must not be above comfort, and comfort must not be above maximum.
    • Thermal mass — the volume of concrete-equivalent mass that stores the heat, in m³. A larger value means the temperature changes more slowly. If you are unsure, start low and raise it if the plan expects the room to cool faster than it really does.
    • Heated floor area — in m².
    • Specific heating demand — the house's yearly heat demand per m², in kWh/m². Often 50 to 150 for a house.
    • Supply temperature — the water temperature sent to the radiators or floor heating. Typically 30 to 35 °C for floor heating and 45 to 55 °C for radiators. A lower value gives a higher COP. Starts at 35.
    • Running sensor / Control entity — optional, as for any thermal load.
    • Advanced settings — the defaults suit most houses:
    • Carnot efficiency — how close the heat pump gets to an ideal one. 0.4 is typical. EMHASS keeps the resulting COP between 1 and 8.
    • Constant COP — optional, 1 to 8. Use a flat COP instead of the calculation, for example the B0/W35 or B0/W45 figure from the manual of a ground-source heat pump, whose COP barely follows the outdoor air. The outdoor temperature then no longer affects the COP, but it still drives the house's heat demand.
    • Source temperature — optional, in °C. The cold-side temperature the COP is calculated from, instead of the outdoor air: a typical brine temperature for a ground-source heat pump. It is a fixed estimate, not a sensor, and must be below the supply temperature. A Constant COP takes precedence. Leave both empty for an air-source heat pump.
    • Base temperature — the outdoor temperature above which the house needs no heating. Starts at 18 °C.
    • Annual heating degree days — the degree days of the year your specific heating demand refers to. Starts at 3000.
    • Thermal inertia — hours before delivered heat shows in the room, for example 1 to 3 for floor heating in concrete. Starts at 0 (immediately).

    After setup¶

    Power, the three temperatures and the comfort times become controls on the load's device page, like for a Room load. The house and heat pump settings are not device controls. To change them, use Edit thermal load, which asks for the name, sensors, control entity and every model setting above. The Heating rate, Heat loss constant and Response delay controls do not exist for this type.

    If the room is too cold to reach its comfort temperature in time, the plan does not fail. It aims for the warmest temperature the heat pump can reach instead, and the Last request to EMHASS sensor's warnings attribute says so. If the room sensor is unavailable, EMHASS starts the plan from 20 °C.

    Getting the numbers right¶

    Compare Planned temperature with the real room temperature over a few days:

    • The planned temperature falls faster than the real one → raise Thermal mass, or lower Specific heating demand.
    • The plan runs more than the heat pump really needs → lower Specific heating demand, or check Supply temperature.
    • Comfort is missed at the start of the window → raise Specific heating demand, or set Thermal inertia if heat takes time to arrive.

    Water tank with an immersion heater¶

    Choose Water tank under Add thermal load. This first version is one tank with one electric immersion element. Room is still the default, and existing loads are never converted based on their temperature targets. Heat pumps, multiple sources, stratification, automatic consumption learning and disinfection schedules are not included.

    Provide tank volume (litres), heater power (watts), a tank temperature sensor, and explicit Lowest standby temperature, Required hot-water temperature and Maximum temperature limits within your hardware's permitted range.

    Choose Number of hot-water periods (1–8), then fill in one page per period: optional name, Hot water needed from / until, and Estimated consumption for this period in kWh of heat withdrawn, not heater electricity. For example, create Morning and Evening periods with separate consumption estimates.

    Water must be ready at each start, with the required tank temperature maintained throughout the period. The lowest standby temperature applies between periods; prices and PV still determine heating times. Zero consumption means no expected withdrawal; availability targets and standby heat loss still apply. Consumption from overlapping periods is added.

    Each period has start/end time controls, a consumption number and an enable switch on the tank device. Changes save immediately and request a fresh optimisation without reloading the integration when the tank load is enabled. While the tank load is disabled, edits save without requesting optimisation. All settings-triggered solves share a three-second delay that restarts with each change; values save immediately, including whole-plan action updates. Disabled periods keep their settings but contribute no withdrawal or required-temperature window. With all periods disabled, only the lowest standby temperature applies.

    Automations can use those entities or replace the whole plan atomically:

    action: emhass_companion.set_hot_water_plan
data:
  device_id: YOUR_TANK_DEVICE_ID
  periods:
    - name: Morning
      draw_start: "06:00:00"
      draw_end: "08:00:00"
      draw_energy_kwh: 3
      enabled: true

    Supply 1–8 periods. Omitted enabled defaults to true. To suspend hot-water availability, disable the periods; an empty plan is rejected. The action validates every period before saving, then requests one optimisation. Adding periods creates their controls live; controls for removed slots become unavailable. Slot numbers and entity IDs stay stable when renamed or disabled.

    Power, temperature limits and Standby loss remain restored device controls. Edit volume, period count and schedules by reconfiguring the tank. Existing periods are prefilled, and changes save only after the final page. Reducing the count keeps the first periods. Legacy single-window tanks run unchanged until edited: their old consumption becomes one period, whose times also set hot-water availability when saved. New tanks have no separate comfort-time controls. Standby loss is continuous heat loss in watts, not hot-water consumption. It is under Advanced settings when the tank is first created and starts at 60 W. The room-model tuning controls do not appear for tanks.

    Heater COP (1 for an immersion element; a heat-pump water heater's seasonal COP otherwise) and Comfort penalty (0 keeps the required temperature a hard limit; above 0 it becomes a priced target) are also restored device controls. Like standby loss, both are offered under Advanced settings when the tank is first created.

    Use the manufacturer's standing loss or calibrate during a no-draw period; compare predicted temperature with the actual sensor before relying on the schedule unattended. A single sensor cannot describe a stratified tank perfectly, and unexpected draws can still exhaust the hot water. Estimated consumption is not automatically learned from temperature changes.

    The tank model needs a known EMHASS 0.18.8 or newer, but no outdoor forecast for immersion heating. Other forecast profiles keep their own requirements. Windows follow local time and may cross midnight. Equal consumption endpoints mean a full day. Daily energy is conserved through daylight-saving changes; partial horizons receive only their overlapping share. Pre-heating ramps use actual elapsed time. ambiguous endpoints use the first occurrence and missing endpoints advance to the next valid local time.

    For enabled tanks, invalid/missing telemetry blocks new solves and switching, including forced-on mode; Should run reports unknown. Existing switch state is left alone, and control resumes only after a valid solve. Hardware thermostats and over-temperature protection remain authoritative. Do not configure two loads for the same physical heater or tank.

    Getting the numbers right¶

    Start with the defaults and adjust from what you observe:

    • Plan heats too early → heat loss constant too high, or heating rate too low.
    • Comfort missed at the start of the window → heating rate too optimistic, or the response delay is real and set to 0.
    • The plan never pre-heats → widen the gap between comfort and maximum temperature; that gap is what it has to play with.
